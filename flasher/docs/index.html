What's different from mainline firmware#

LR2021 radios (X1, AB Labs XIAO and WisBlock NiceRF, Meshnology W12)#

These use or change the LR2021 driver, so they only affect boards with that chip.

  • Meshtastic channel. With mt on, the radio also listens to Meshtastic LongFast and shows its texts in the app as one more channel. Receive only; the radio chip hops between the two networks by itself (see below). beala/main
  • Weather sensors. With wx on, readings from Ecowitt, Ambient Weather, other Fine Offset and La Crosse View sensors show in the app as a "Weather" channel. The radio steps away from the mesh only around each sensor's next packet (see below). beala/main
  • Aircraft. With air on, aircraft reporting on 978 MHz (UAT) and 1090 MHz (ADS-B) show in an "Aircraft" channel: a digest of the ones nearby every 10 minutes (see below). beala/main
  • Airport weather. With metar on, METARs and TAFs that FAA ground stations broadcast on 978 MHz (FIS-B) show in plain words in an "Airport weather" channel (see below). beala/main
  • Utility meters. With meter on, your own water, gas and electric meters' readings (Neptune R900, Itron ERT) show in a "Meters" channel, with the water meter's leak flags (see below). beala/main
  • Tire pressure. With tpms on, your car's tire pressure sensors (Ford, Jeep, Toyota, GM, Honda, Hyundai/Kia) show in a "Tires" channel, with low and dropping pressure alerts (see below). beala/main
  • Radar sweep detector. radar listens on 1030 MHz for the pulses ground radars send to aircraft and works out how fast the radars nearby are turning. Nothing is sent (see below). beala/main
  • One at a time. Meshtastic, weather and aircraft each take some of the radio's time away from the mesh, so only one is on at a time: turning one on turns the others off, and the reply says so. radar, copying a radio and find range pause whichever is on until they finish. After you send, no listen starts until the repeats of what you sent are in. beala/main
  • Full transmit power. RadioLib's power table for the LR2021 stops rising at about 14 dBm, whatever you set. This uses Semtech's reference table, so settings up to 22 dBm give the power they say. beala/fix-lr2021-pa-table
  • Semtech firmware patch. Semtech says to load a patch into the radio after every reset; RadioLib never does. It's now loaded at boot. beala/lr2021-pram-patch
  • AGC reset fix. With agc.reset.interval turned on, the radio was left asleep after each reset: packets were missed and queued messages didn't send. It now wakes and recalibrates properly. beala/fix-lr2021-agc-reset
  • Correct boosted-gain status. The boot log always said RX boosted gain was off, even when it was on. beala/fix-lr2021-rx-boost-status
  • SIMO power supply (X1 and AB Labs XIAO). The radio runs from its built-in DC-DC converter, which uses less current. beala/lr2021-simo-flag, beala/x1-simo
  • Radio diagnostics. The two commands below, plus transmit fault logging in debug builds. beala/lr2021-tx-fault-log, beala/lr2021-rx-gain-step, beala/lr2021-diagnostics

All boards#

Several companions with one key. Pick one radio as the main radio; the others with the same key still show the messages they hear but never answer them, so senders get one reply and one path, and "delivered" means the main radio has it. A Radio console contact in the app runs radio commands and tells you when another radio with your key is heard (see below). beala/main

Read damaged packets. A message that arrives with a few bad bits is normally thrown away. With dmg on, a companion reads what came through and shows it in a "Damaged" channel, with ▒▒▒▒ where text was lost (see below). beala/main

Resend messages that don't get out. After sending a message, the radio listens for the next repeater passing it on. If it hears none, it sends the exact same packet once more. Radios that already have it ignore the copy, so nobody sees it twice. Works for channel messages and for direct messages along a path. On by default on companions, off by default on repeaters (see below). beala/flood-retry

Commands in a chat. Type /range, /crypto v2 or /path in a DM, or /crypto in a channel, and the radio runs it for that chat instead of sending it. The reply shows in the same chat (see below). beala/main

INA219 shunt setting. The INA219 current sensor's readings assumed a 0.1 ohm shunt resistor. Set yours with sensor set ina219_shunt <ohms> (for example 0.01 for a board marked R010). It's saved and survives a reboot. sensor get ina219_shunt shows it; it only appears in sensor list if an INA219 was found at boot. The most it can measure is 0.32 V divided by the shunt: 3.2 A with 0.1 ohm, 32 A with 0.01 ohm. beala/ina219-shunt

Safer randomness for new keys. A radio now only keeps a new private key if it gathered enough randomness from sources it can trust, and the Heltec V3 and V4 use their chip's random generator properly (see below). beala/entropy

Packet parser security fixes. Neil Alexander's fixes from meshcore-dev/MeshCore#3521, not yet merged upstream. Malformed or truncated packets from the air could make the firmware read past the end of buffers or use stale memory; the parser now rejects them. Covers empty and short frames, partial encrypted payloads, oversized advert data, bad TRACE and CONTROL packets, and ACK overflows. One addition on top: packets with a newer, unsupported protocol version are dropped instead of being handled as version 1. beala/pr-3521

Find a lost radio#

Make a radio beep so you can find it by ear, from the radio itself or from another one. Between two radios with an LR2021 (such as two X1s) you can also measure how far apart they are.

How to use it#

  1. Set it up once, before anything gets lost. You need two radios that have each other as contacts. On the radio you might lose, run set find.allow <other radio's name>. Do the same the other way round if either could get lost.
  2. Lost, but you can still reach it from the app (for example over Bluetooth): run find me on it. It beeps for 2 minutes, even with the buzzer turned off, and vibrates on the X1. Press its button or run find me stop to end it.
  3. Lost for real: on the other radio, run find beep <name>, wait a few seconds, then get find. You should see beeping for 120 s. find stop <name> silences it.
  4. Both radios have an LR2021 (X1, AB Labs XIAO and WisBlock NiceRF, Meshnology W12): run find range <name>, then get find, for something like 42 m (+/- 4 m, 30/32 answers, -97 dBm). Move and repeat: if the number goes down, you're getting closer.

If it doesn't work#

  • nothing yet: the lost radio didn't hear you. It has to be in range (through repeaters is fine for beeping; ranging needs a direct path). Get closer and try again.
  • find: not allowed: step 1 wasn't done on the lost radio.
  • old request ignored (is your clock set?): the radio you're using has the wrong time. Connect it to the app so its clock gets set.
  • ranging didn't start: this radio was busy transmitting: run find range again. (Builds before 4 Nov said radio busy here even when the radio was free; update both radios.)
  • ranging needs 902-928 MHz: ranging uses a 500 kHz wide signal, which only fits the US band (also Canada, Australia, New Zealand).

Technical details#

  • Requests are encrypted CLI messages to the target, like a direct message. The target only acts on them from contacts on its allow list (or with remote CLI access), and only if the request is newer than the last one it accepted from that contact, so recorded requests can't be replayed. The list is saved in the find section of /prefs.json.
  • Ranging uses the LR2021's round-trip time-of-flight mode: SF9, 500 kHz, up to 32 exchanges in 2.5 s, reporting the median. Command sequence and bench results from beala/lr2021-re. The distance offset isn't calibrated, so expect it to be a few metres off; walls and bodies make it read longer.
  • While ranging, the lost radio is busy for 5 s and the finder for up to 2.5 s. Neither hears mesh traffic in that time.
  • Each ranging session uses a random address, sent only inside the encrypted reply, so other radios can't easily answer in its place or range the lost radio while it listens. The ranging exchange itself isn't encrypted, so a determined attacker could still fake the distance.
  • Extended ranging runs two exchanges per measurement whose errors from relative motion are opposite: their average gives the distance, their difference the speed. In normal mode, motion can throw the distance off by tens of metres.

Full write-up: docs/find_me.md. Source: beala/find-me.

Flood retry#

When you send a message, your radio listens for the next repeater passing it on. If it hears nothing within about 2.5 seconds, it sends the same packet again, byte for byte, down to the timestamp. That covers channel messages, messages to contacts it doesn't have a path to yet, and direct messages that follow a path through repeaters. For a direct message, a lost hop is fixed in a few seconds instead of waiting for the app to time out and retry.

That's safe because every radio and repeater remembers the messages it has seen and drops repeats of them. A repeater that got the first copy ignores the resend, and so do the radios that showed you the message. A repeater that missed the first copy treats the resend as new and passes it on. So the resend only fixes the case where the first hop was lost, and costs one extra transmission when it wasn't needed.

On a repeater it works the same way for messages it forwards: if it doesn't hear the next hop pass a message on, it sends its copy again. That helps on every hop of a direct route, and where one repeater is the only link between two areas. It's off by default. Adverts are never resent, and a resend is skipped while the repeater has other packets waiting. A repeater at the edge of the mesh, with nobody further out, would resend every channel message once, so leave it off there.

One key, several radios#

You can load the same private key onto more than one companion, say one at home and one you carry, and both show your messages. With more than one switched on, a message that arrives by flood reaches each of them and each replies. The sender gets two replies and two paths, keeps the last, and sends its direct messages to that one radio from then on.

With this firmware, one radio can take over as the main radio. The others become backups: they still show the messages they hear, but send no acknowledgement, path or response. Senders only ever hear from the main radio, however far apart your radios are. If the main radio is off or out of range, nothing answers and the sender sees the message fail, so take over on the radio you have with you. If you send a message from a backup, its console offers to make it the main radio.

Copy a radio#

To set up a second radio with your key, copy your radio onto it: key, contacts, channels, encryption policies and settings. Put the two radios next to each other. On the new radio send clone receive: it shows a 6-digit code and waits 5 minutes. Then, on the radio you're copying, send clone send and the code. A few seconds later it says "Copied to the other radio: 17 KB in 1.5 s over FLRC", and the new radio restarts with your key as a backup. The radio you copied from becomes the main radio. The new radio keeps its own transmit power, receiver gain, BLE PIN and GPS settings. Each radio needs an LR2021 or an SX1262, in any mix.

The code protects the copy with CPace, a password-authenticated key exchange (IRTF CFRG, X25519). Someone listening can't test guesses of the code, and a radio pretending to be the other one gets one guess. The radios start in GFSK (250 kbps), which both chips have, on 920 MHz in the US. Two LR2021s then move to the LR2021's faster FLRC mode (1.3 Mbps). The data is encrypted with ChaCha20-Poly1305, and the new radio checks the whole copy before replacing anything, so a failed copy leaves it as it was.

The Radio console contact#

Every radio with this firmware adds a contact called Radio console to the app. It isn't a real node, and messages to it never go on air: the radio runs them as commands and replies in the same chat. Send help to start. Commands aren't case sensitive. Other radio commands work there too, such as get flood.retry, so you can use them from an app that has no command line.

The console also tells you things. When your radio hears another radio with your key, it posts: "Another radio with this key is active: it sent to Bob. Make this the main radio, so the others stay quiet? Reply "yes"."

Meshtastic channel#

A radio with an LR2021 (X1, AB Labs XIAO and WisBlock NiceRF, Meshnology W12) can listen to a Meshtastic channel as well as MeshCore. Meshtastic texts show up in the MeshCore app as one more channel, "Meshtastic LongFast", after your own. The app needs no changes. Nothing is ever sent on Meshtastic, and nothing heard there is passed on to MeshCore.

How to use it#

  1. In the Radio console (or the app's command line), run mt on.
  2. Disconnect and reconnect the app. A channel named Meshtastic LongFast appears.
  3. Texts arrive as Name: text. The sender shows as !a1b2c3d4 until the radio hears their node info, then by name. Long texts are split over several messages.

By default it listens to the public US LongFast channel: 906.875 MHz, default key. Text typed into the Meshtastic channel isn't sent; the radio answers that the channel is receive-only.

What it doesn't do#

  • Send anything on Meshtastic, or show DMs, positions or telemetry.
  • Hear MeshCore while it's receiving a Meshtastic packet (a LongFast text takes up to 2 s on air). On a busy LongFast mesh this costs some MeshCore packets.

Technical details#

  • The LR2021's own CPU hops between MeshCore and Meshtastic, each with its own frequency, modulation, preamble and sync word, from a small program loaded into its patch RAM. It checks each channel for a preamble about every 20 ms and stays on the first one with a packet. The phone-side processor sleeps through it (95% idle while scanning).
  • While Meshtastic is on, that program takes the place of Semtech's patch in the radio. mt off puts the patch back.
  • Transmits always use MeshCore's settings: the hopping stops first and resumes after.
  • Bench, an hour of random traffic: 1051/1052 MeshCore and 1054/1055 Meshtastic packets received; a later fix recovers the remaining misses.
  • Decoding is written from the on-air format (header, channel hash, AES-CTR, protobuf); no Meshtastic code is used.

Full write-up: docs/meshtastic_channel.md. Source: beala/main.

Weather sensors#

A radio with an LR2021 (X1, AB Labs XIAO and WisBlock NiceRF, Meshnology W12) can pick up wireless weather sensors from Ecowitt, Ambient Weather, other Fine Offset models (Froggit, Misol, ...), La Crosse (View and IT+) and ThermoPro on 915 MHz. Their readings show in the MeshCore app as one more channel, "Weather". Nothing is sent, and the app needs no changes.

How to use it#

  1. In the Radio console (or the app's command line), run wx on. The radio listens for sensors for about 6 minutes (it doesn't hear the mesh during that time), then lists what it found in the console.
  2. Disconnect and reconnect the app. A channel named Weather appears after your own, in a fixed slot after Meshtastic's (empty if that's off).
  3. Each sensor posts its first reading, then one every 30 minutes, for example WH65 5a: 68.0F 50% wind 6 N gust 10 mph rain 1.00 in UV 0.0 1234 lux. Lightning, the start of rain and a leak sensor finding water post straight away.

Looking for sensors, e.g. driving around: wx scan on listens all the time and posts each new sensor to the console the moment it's heard. The mesh isn't heard meanwhile (sending still works); wx scan off goes back to normal.

Sensors covered: every model rtl_433 reads in these radio formats at 915 MHz. Ecowitt/Ambient WH65 and WS-2902 outdoor arrays (and the WS69's pressure), WH31/WN31 thermo-hygrometers, WN32B/WH32B/WH32 outdoor and WH25 indoor sensors, WH40 and WN20 rain, WH57 lightning, WS68/WS80/WS85/WS90, WN34/WN38 probes, WH41/WH43/WH45/WH46 air quality, WH51/WH52 soil, WH55 leak; La Crosse View LTV-TH2/TH3, LTV-WSDTH01 Breeze Pro, LTV-W1/W2 and LTV-WR1 wind, LTV-R1 rain; La Crosse IT+ (TX29-IT, TX25U-IT, TX35-IT), TX22U-IT and TX31U-IT; ThermoPro TX-7B. Each was checked against rtl_433's recordings of real sensors where there are any (152 of 152 readings the same). Not yet: 433 MHz on-off keyed sensors (Acurite, Ambient F007TH) and Davis.

In the app. The fluxcore app's Sensors tab lists every sensor heard with its latest readings. Search and filter by name, ID or model; star the ones to post (it sets wx.only); give them names and hide the neighbours'; and see each one's readings over time (kept in your browser, saved as CSV).

Something else nearby? A packet that checks out the way these formats' packets do but isn't a known model is kept: get wx.unknown lists them (so does the app), with the bytes needed to add the model.

Technical details#

  • The sensors send short FSK packets (Ecowitt 17.24 kbit/s; La Crosse and ThermoPro at six rates from 8.6 to 9.6 kbit/s. The radio takes a rate only within about 0.2% of its setting, so each listen is set to the rate measured on recordings of real sensors) at a fixed interval: 16 s for an outdoor array, 61 s for a WH31. The radio learns each interval and switches from LoRa to FSK for about 0.3 s around each expected packet, then straight back. A sensor that goes quiet is hunted with short listens at random gaps.
  • Mesh traffic comes first: a listen waits for an incoming mesh packet, and a transmit ends a listen.
  • Bench, 20 minutes with three sensors and MeshCore traffic: every sensor packet heard; about 1% of the time away from the mesh while tracking; no MeshCore packets lost after the start-up scan.

Aircraft#

Aircraft report their position about once a second over ADS-B: light aircraft in the US on 978 MHz (UAT), airliners and most others on 1090 MHz. A radio with an LR2021 (X1, AB Labs XIAO and WisBlock NiceRF, Meshnology W12) listens to both in short breaks from the mesh. Every 10 minutes the "Aircraft" channel gets a digest of the aircraft within 10 miles and below 10,000 ft, closest first: Nearby: N713SA 5,300 ft 2.1 mi N; N25430 6,100 ft 4.0 mi NE (+12 higher or farther). Nothing is ever sent on 978 or 1090 MHz.

  1. In the Radio console, run air on (listens 10% of the time), or air scan on to listen all the time with a console note for each new aircraft.
  2. Disconnect and reconnect the app. A channel named Aircraft appears.
  3. Set your location in the app for distance and direction (and for the 10-mile limit).

On a map. Open the fluxcore app in Chrome or Edge (computer or Android) or Bluefy (iPhone, iPad), connect to the radio over Bluetooth, and every aircraft it hears shows on a map with its trail, altitude, speed and heading, updated every 2 seconds. The app talks to the radio on its own Bluetooth channel, so the MeshCore app can stay connected (on an iPhone, both at once). If the app says it isn't on that channel, forget the radio in your Bluetooth settings and pair it again.

Damaged packets#

A packet that arrives with a few bad bits fails the radio's checksum, and MeshCore throws it away. With dmg on, a companion keeps it and reads what came through. The messages show in the MeshCore app as one more channel, "Damaged", with ▒▒▒▒ where text was lost. Any radio, LR2021 or not.

Bob: [#Public, 1 of 5 lost] running late, be there ▒▒▒▒ minutes
Alice: [#Public, fixed] anyone on the hill tonight?
Carol: [direct, 2 of 6 lost] ▒▒▒▒the gate code is 4471, ▒▒▒▒

Run dmg on, then disconnect and reconnect the app. The channel appears after your own, in a fixed slot after Meshtastic, Weather and Aircraft.

  • Why it works. MeshCore scrambles a message in 16-character pieces, each on its own. Bad bits spoil only the pieces they land in, and the rest still unscramble to the right text. The radio tries each channel key it has, and for a direct message the contacts whose ID matches, and keeps the pieces that come out as text.
  • Fixed. When only one piece is bad, the radio tries flipping each of its bits until the message's check code matches. Copies of a flood message heard through different repeaters are joined, so one copy's good pieces fill the other's gaps.
  • Not shown twice. If any copy arrives whole, the damaged one isn't shown. Neither is a damaged echo of your own message. set dmg.all on shows them anyway, labelled "also heard whole".
  • Shown only. A damaged message can't be checked as genuine, so it never goes in the chat it was for, and is never acknowledged, passed on, or used to update a contact.

dmg off		Start or stop (saved)
set dmg.all on
set dmg.all off		Show everything (saved; default off): also damaged copies of messages that arrived whole, your own echoes, and each damaged packet that couldn't be read, with what it was (advert, ack, someone else's message...), its signal, and the radio that sent it

All of this is guesswork from damaged bytes, each guess with a rule. A piece counts as read when it decrypts to text (noise passes about once in 87,000 tries), and the first piece also needs a sensible timestamp. A packet's type comes from its unencrypted first byte, checked against its size; who sent it, from the last repeater in its path, shown as its ID in hex (a contact's name is added only when 2 or more bytes of ID match: 1 in 256 radios share a 1-byte ID). Every rule, its numbers and how it can go wrong: How it decides.

Encryption v2 (experimental)#

Opt-in, per contact and per channel. It's new and hasn't been reviewed independently yet, so don't rely on it for anything sensitive.

In plain terms#

A private message's encryption has two jobs: scramble it so listeners can't read it, and seal it so the receiver knows it really came from you and wasn't changed or made up.

Why MeshCore's current encryption is weak:

  • It leaks patterns. Messages are scrambled in 16-character pieces, and the same piece always scrambles the same way. A listener can't read your words, but can tell when you repeat them, like a code where each common word always gets the same codeword.
  • The seal is far too short. It's 2 bytes, so there are only 65,536 possible seals. Someone without the key can keep sending fakes until about 1 in 65,536 is accepted, and because pieces are scrambled separately, they can build the fake by cutting and pasting pieces of your earlier messages, so it reads as real text.

What v2 changes: it uses AES-SIV, a published standard (RFC 5297), not a home-made scheme.

  • No patterns. The whole message is scrambled together, so repeated words no longer look alike.
  • A seal that can't be guessed. 16 bytes instead of 2: guessing one is less likely than winning a 1-in-300-million lottery four times in a row.
  • No cut and paste. The seal covers the whole message plus who it's from and to. Change any part and the message is thrown away.

Working with radios that don't have it: repeaters relay v2 like any other message, and radios without v2 quietly ignore it. Everything starts on the old format. You switch a contact or channel to v2 once the other side has it. When a contact sends you v2, your radio starts replying in v2 by itself, but nothing ever switches back to the old format automatically. v2only refuses the old format from that contact or channel entirely.

Cost: about 7 more bytes per message on average. 160-character texts still fit.

What it doesn't fix: channel members can still read everything and post under any name; public and hashtag channels aren't private; someone who records messages and later steals your radio's key can read them; recorded messages can be replayed; and who's talking, how often and how much is still visible.

Technical details#

  • Cipher: RFC 5297 AES-SIV with AES-128 and a 16-byte synthetic IV/tag. Key = HKDF-SHA256 of the existing 32-byte shared or channel secret. Associated data: the label "MeshCore AEAD v2", then the payload type and the fixed addressing fields (destination/source hashes, sender key for anonymous requests, or the channel hash). Fields repeaters change (path, hop count) aren't covered.
  • Telling formats apart: an old-format payload is always 2 + 16·n bytes. v2 never uses that length (one 0x00 byte is added when it would), so the receiver picks the format from the length alone, and firmware from v1.18.0 on always rejects v2. The packet header is unchanged, so old repeaters keep forwarding.
  • Downgrades: automatic changes only ever raise a contact to v2; channels never change on their own; a missing ACK never falls back. Policies are saved to two alternating checksummed files, and prefs record the newest save. If it can't be loaded at boot, everything is treated as v2only until set crypto.recover, instead of silently accepting the old format again.
  • Payload safety: the receive buffer past the decrypted message is zeroed. PATH payloads whose path doesn't fit are rejected (an over-read that also affects mainline). Tests run under AddressSanitizer with random payloads of every type.
  • Timing: on nRF52, AES runs on the CC310 hardware engine, which has no secret-dependent lookup tables and is faster. It's checked against an RFC test vector on first use, with software AES as the fallback. ESP32 already uses its AES hardware. The tag comparison is constant-time (checked in the ARM compiler output). A packet built to trigger the padding retry costs two decryption attempts instead of one, which is CPU time only and leaks nothing. An automatic upgrade is written to flash 5 seconds later, so it doesn't delay the first v2 message's ACK.
  • Overhead: 16 bytes (+1 in 1 of 16 lengths) against v1's 2 + 0–15 bytes of padding: about 6.6 bytes more on average. Longest direct message 165 bytes (v1: 167).

Full specification, test vectors and limitations: docs/crypto_v2.md.

Key randomness#

In plain terms#

The first time a radio starts, it makes its own private key: a secret number that everything private hangs off. Every direct message to or from that radio is protected with it. The key is only as good as the randomness used to pick it. If that randomness were predictable, someone could work out the key and read those messages.

What was wrong:

  • On the Heltec V3 and V4 (ESP32), the chip's good random generator was switched off by a quirk in how the firmware starts up. The key came only from the LoRa radio's noise reading, which nobody has checked.
  • On every board, nothing checked whether the sources were working. If they all failed, the radio would still make a key and keep it forever.

What changed:

  • Only proven sources count. The chip's generator counts only when it reports success and is known to be truly random at that moment. Radio noise counts only if it passes standard checks for a stuck or lopsided signal, and then at a fraction of what it probably holds. Timing is mixed in but counts for nothing, because start-up timing can repeat from boot to boot.
  • No key is kept without enough. A key is saved only once 256 bits of trusted randomness have been gathered. Otherwise the radio uses a temporary key for this session and tries again at the next boot. It never keeps a key that might be weak.
  • ESP32 turns its noise source on. Right at power-on, before anything else starts, the chip briefly switches on the internal noise source its random generator needs.
  • A standard generator. Keys come from HMAC-DRBG, the NIST-standard random generator, checked against NIST's official test vectors.

Which radios it affects: it's a real fix on the Heltec V3 and V4 and the Meshnology W12. The T1, X1, T1000-E, WisMesh Tag, RAK3401 and AB Labs XIAO already used their chip's hardware generator, and now also check that it worked.

Your existing key doesn't change. Updating the firmware keeps it. To get a key made the new way, do a full erase when flashing. That gives the radio a new identity, so your contacts will need to add it again. Run get entropy afterwards to check there was enough randomness.

The random 6-digit Bluetooth PIN, shown on boards with a screen, comes from the same generator, and every PIN is equally likely. Channel keys aren't made on the radio: the phone app picks them. Hashtag channels aren't private at all.

Technical details#

  • Before: each key byte was the radio's randomByte() XOR Arduino random(), plus the CC310 TRNG on nRF52. On ESP32, randomSeed() at boot switches random() to the newlib PRNG, seeded from 31 bits of the same radio. Failures were ignored and a key was always saved.
  • Now: inputs are conditioned into a SHA-256 pool, which seeds an HMAC-DRBG (SP 800-90A, SHA-256, tested against 20 NIST CAVP vectors). Keys are kept only when at least 256 bits have been credited:
    • CC310: 256 bits, only if generate() reports success.
    • ESP32 RNG: 256 bits, only in collectEarly(), the first call in every firmware's setup(). It runs with the SAR ADC entropy source on (bootloader_random_enable()), before the ADC or RF start, as ESP-IDF requires. At any other time the ESP32 RNG counts for nothing.
    • Radio noise: 1 bit per sample, for 512-sample batches that pass the SP 800-90B repetition count and adaptive proportion tests. These catch a stuck or biased radio, not a predictable one.
    • Timings, seeds and anything else: 0.
  • Fail closed: the companion, repeater, room server, sensor, KISS modem and terminal chat firmware all save a new identity only if there was enough. The terminal chat example used to make keys from the Arduino PRNG seeded at a keypress.
  • Other details: the BLE PIN uses rejection sampling, temporaries are wiped with a volatile write, and the pool is documented as single-threaded.
  • Cost: about 0.6 s on first boot with trusted hardware. Without it (RP2040, STM32, ESP32-C6), about 5 s per 512-sample batch on an SX126x, and up to 3 batches.
  • Limits: no hardware has been measured yet. The ESP32 credit relies on ESP-IDF's statement about the SAR ADC source. RP2040 and STM32 depend on radio noise alone.

Full write-up: docs/entropy.md. Source: beala/entropy.

The receiver gain step the radio picked for each of the last packets, with their signal strength, SNR and age, newest first.

> G13 -112dBm -6.5dB 4s; G9 -87dBm 8.0dB 31s

Higher gain steps mean the radio amplified a weaker signal. Useful for spotting a receiver that's being overloaded by a nearby transmitter (low gain steps on weak packets). Before anything is received: > no packets received yet

get nodes.freq#

Every radio's crystal is a little off frequency, by its own steady amount, and the LR2021 measures it on each packet. The radio keeps a table of the radios around it, one row each, with that frequency as a fingerprint:

> 3 radios; a2b3: +27Hz ~9, 19, 6s; 4c (not 4c07): +23Hz ~9, 12, 5s; 4c07 Sender: -390Hz ~2, 6, 7s

Per radio: its ID, its usual frequency error, how far its readings stray, packets heard and when last heard. A radio gets 2 or 3 bytes of ID and a name only once a signed advert or an ID of 2 bytes or more confirms it; until then it shows its 1-byte ID. 4c (not 4c07) means a radio heard only by the 1-byte ID 4c, at another frequency than 4c07, so a different radio that happens to share the byte. Damaged packets that don't say who sent them are placed by frequency too, shown with a ~ (~a2b3: [channel message, -10dB] ...). Radios less than about 40 Hz apart can't be told apart, and it isn't security: a sender can shift their frequency on purpose. The fluxcore app's Radios tab draws them on one frequency axis, with each radio's drift since it connected. get radio.freq lists each packet's reading; nodes.freq reset clears the table. Write-up: docs/frequency_fingerprint.md.

How the builds are made#

The builds come from beala/main, the fork's own main line. Every 30 minutes, and whenever beala/main changes, a GitHub workflow in beala/MeshCore merges in the newest upstream dev, builds all boards in parallel, and publishes a release tagged beala-<commit>. This site checks for new releases every 15 minutes and republishes.

The release notes list what changed since the previous release, with a link to the full diff against upstream. The branch names next to each feature above are where that change is kept for sending upstream.
